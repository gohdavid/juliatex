\title{Inferring Molecular Dynamics and Force Fields from Equilibrium Distributions}

\author{Rachit Mukkamala, David Goh}
\affiliation{Harvard-MIT Program in Health Sciences and Technology}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Equilibrium structural ensembles describe which molecular conformations occur but do not directly provide the forces needed to simulate motion.
Statistical mechanics connects these views: gradients of the equilibrium log density determine conservative force information.
We study this idea in controlled Rouse-chain polymer systems where the true equilibrium score is known analytically, with protein dynamics as the motivating application.
We test whether continuous normalizing flows (CNFs) and diffusion models can recover both conformational statistics and force information from static samples alone.
Both model classes improve over Gaussian baselines, but score recovery is most reliable for simple harmonic chains and degrades when ring closure or nonlocal contacts shape the ensemble.
These benchmarks clarify what equilibrium-density learning can and cannot yet provide for molecular dynamics inference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}

Protein conformational dynamics motivate this work.
Experimental and computational methods can provide structural ensembles, but those ensembles typically lack the time ordering, transition paths, or effective force fields needed to reconstruct dynamics \cite{noe2019,arts2023}.
Statistical mechanics offers a route from equilibrium density to forces: in a canonical ensemble the conformational density is proportional to $\exp[-U(x)/(k_B T)]$, so gradients of the log density carry force information.
Additional dynamical assumptions then determine how to use that information for trajectory simulation.

Directly testing this idea on proteins is difficult because the equilibrium distribution is only partially observed and no clean score or force-field target is available.
We therefore study controlled Brownian-dynamics systems based on Rouse-chain polymers \cite{rouse1953}.
These are not protein models; they are tractable benchmarks where the simulator provides both equilibrium samples and the corresponding analytic score field.
They let us test whether continuous normalizing flows and diffusion models learn both the equilibrium distribution and the local log-density gradients that, in this Brownian setting, govern overdamped dynamics \cite{grathwohl2018,arts2023}.

For a configuration $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times N}$ with $N$ beads in $d$ spatial dimensions, we simulate the dynamics
\begin{equation}
    dX_t = F(X_t)\,dt + \sqrt{2D}\,dW_t,
\end{equation}
where $D$ denotes the diffusion coefficient, $W_t$ denotes standard Brownian motion, and $F(x)$ denotes the deterministic drift.
We set $D=0.25$ in the base Rouse simulations.
Adjacent harmonic springs generate the drift,
\begin{equation}
    F_i(x)
    =
    k_{\xi}
    \left(
        x_{i+1} - 2x_i + x_{i-1}
    \right),
\end{equation}
with endpoint modifications.
The simulator also supports nonideal terms: Lennard-Jones interactions, excluded volume, confinement, hairpin contacts, and ring closure.

At equilibrium, the stationary density score determines the drift:
\begin{equation}
    F(x) = D \nabla_x \log p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x).
\end{equation}
We use this relation as a test: if a model learns $p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)$, its score should match the Brownian drift divided by $D$.
For the base setting this gives the score target $F(x)/0.25 = 4F(x)$.
We learn equilibrium coordinate distributions from static snapshots and then compare both generated samples and learned score fields against known Rouse-chain quantities.

\section{Overdamped Langevin and Detailed Balance}

We use overdamped Brownian dynamics rather than inertial molecular dynamics.
Solvent friction damps momenta faster than the configurational motion we model, so the state is the bead-coordinate vector $x$ rather than a position-momentum pair.
For a physical coordinate vector $\vec{q}$ with potential energy $V(\vec{q})$, the overdamped Langevin SDE has the form
\begin{equation}
    d\vec{q}_t
    =
    -\mu
    \nabla_{\vec{q}}V(\vec{q}_t)\,dt
    +
    \sqrt{2D}\,dW_t,
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is the mobility and $D=\mu k_B T$ is the diffusion coefficient.
The corresponding Boltzmann equilibrium density is
\begin{equation}
    P(\vec{q})
    =
    \frac{1}{Z}
    \exp
    \left(
        -\frac{V(\vec{q})}{k_B T}
    \right),
\end{equation}
with partition function
\begin{equation}
    Z
    =
    \int
    \exp
    \left(
        -\frac{V(\vec{q})}{k_B T}
    \right)
    d\vec{q}.
\end{equation}
The Fokker-Planck probability current for the general overdamped SDE
\begin{equation}
    dX_t
    =
    F(X_t)\,dt
    +
    \sqrt{2D}\,dW_t
\end{equation}
is
\begin{equation}
    J(x)
    =
    F(x)p(x)
    -
    D\nabla_x p(x).
\end{equation}
Detailed balance sets this current to zero pointwise at equilibrium:
\begin{equation}
    J_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)=0.
\end{equation}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
    F(x)p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)
    =
    D\nabla_x p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x),
\end{equation}
and dividing by $p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)$ gives
\begin{equation}
    F(x)
    =
    D\nabla_x\log p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x).
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_x\log p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)
    =
    \frac{F(x)}{D}.
\end{equation}
We use this detailed-balance relation as the score target throughout.
Any model that evaluates or differentiates the equilibrium log density can be tested against the analytic drift supplied by the simulator \cite{noe2019,arts2023}.

\section{Data}

The training data consist of centered bead configurations from simulated Rouse trajectories \cite{rouse1953}, stored as HDF5 arrays of shape
\begin{equation}
    x \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times N \times B},
\end{equation}
where $B$ denotes the number of frames.
We simulate two-dimensional chains with $N=32$ beads.
The base simulation uses $D=0.25$, unit bond length, and $k_\xi=3.0$; the ring-closure and hairpin configurations inherit these constants and add structural constraints.
We center each frame before training so that the models learn internal conformations rather than global translation.

\section{Time Conventions}

We use two distinct time variables.
Physical simulation time $t$ indexes the Brownian dynamics and all generated polymer trajectories.
Model pseudotime $\tau \in [0,1]$ indexes the generative models: $\tau=0$ is the data distribution and $\tau=1$ is the standard normal base.
The CNF integrates from $\tau=0$ to $\tau=1$ for likelihood evaluation and reverses for sampling.
The diffusion model noises data from $\tau=0$ toward Gaussian noise at $\tau=1$ and denoises in the reverse direction.

\section{Synthetic Systems}

We study three controlled synthetic systems: a base Rouse chain, a ring-closure Rouse chain, and a hairpin-stabilized Rouse chain \cite{rouse1953,honda2004,satoh2006}.
The base system tests whether a model can learn the equilibrium distribution and score of a flexible harmonic polymer.
The ring-closure system adds a single terminal bond, changing the chain topology while keeping the force law nearly identical.
The hairpin system adds attractive nonlocal contacts and repulsive excluded volume.
It is not a protein model, but it provides a minimal analogue of a folded motif: the chain fluctuates thermally while long-range contacts stabilize a preferred conformation.

\subsection*{Base Rouse Chain}

The base Rouse model uses a quadratic bond potential between adjacent beads \cite{rouse1953}.
The dimensionless equilibrium potential is
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{bond}}(x)
    =
    \frac{k_\xi}{2D}
    \sum_{i=2}^{N}
    \|x_i - x_{i-1}\|^2.
\end{equation}
The corresponding equilibrium score satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_x \log p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)
    =
    -
    \nabla_x U_{\mathrm{bond}}(x),
\end{equation}
and the Brownian drift satisfies $F(x)=D\nabla_x\log p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)$.
We use $N=32$ beads in two dimensions with $D=0.25$ and $k_\xi=3.0$, and disable all nonideal interactions.
The base Rouse chain provides a clean baseline: adjacent harmonic springs generate the entire equilibrium score.

\subsection*{Nonideal Interactions}

The simulator supports several nonideal potentials.
The nonbonded Lennard-Jones potential has the form
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{LJ}}(r)
    =
    4\epsilon
    \left[
        \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12}
        -
        \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^6
    \right],
\end{equation}
with optional cutoff, softening, bonded-neighbor exclusion, and energy shifting.
The force routine uses softened squared distance $r^2+\delta^2$ for numerical stability.

Excluded volume uses a purely repulsive power-law potential,
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{EV}}(r)
    =
    \epsilon_{\mathrm{EV}}
    \left(
        \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{EV}}}{r}
    \right)^{p},
\end{equation}
again with cutoff, softening, and bonded-neighbor exclusion.
We use the defaults
\begin{align}
    \epsilon_{\mathrm{EV}}=0.03,
    \quad
    \sigma_{\mathrm{EV}}=1.0,
    \quad
    p=12,
    \nonumber \\
    \delta=0.3,
    \quad
    r_{\mathrm{cut}}=1.5.
\end{align}
This term prevents bead overlap while preserving chain flexibility.

\subsection*{Ring-Closure Rouse Chain}

The ring-closure system starts from a circular initialization and evolves with Brownian dynamics.
For unit bond length, the initializer places $N$ beads on a circle of radius
\begin{equation}
    R
    =
    \frac{1}{2\sin(\pi/N)}
\end{equation}
and adds Gaussian noise with standard deviation $0.02$ before centering.
We use $N=32$, $D=0.25$, $k_\xi=3.0$, and unit bond length as in the base chain.

A harmonic closure bond between beads $1$ and $N$ adds the ring-specific potential:
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{ring}}(x)
    =
    U_{\mathrm{bond}}(x)
    +
    \frac{k_\xi}{2D}
    \|x_1-x_N\|^2.
\end{equation}
This closure uses the same spring constant as the adjacent Rouse bonds.
The configuration also enables excluded volume and centered confinement with the constants listed above; the optional ring Lennard-Jones contact is disabled.
The ring experiment tests whether the model can learn a closed-polymer equilibrium distribution.

\subsection*{Hairpin-Stabilized Chain}

The hairpin system starts from a two-strand initialization: the first half of the chain lies on one strand and the second half on the opposing strand, with paired beads facing each other.
We set the initial strand separation to $0.95$ and add Gaussian noise with standard deviation $0.02$.

Attractive Lennard-Jones contacts between paired beads stabilize the hairpin:
\begin{equation}
    i+j=N+1,
    \qquad
    |i-j| \geq s_{\min}.
\end{equation}
Only these long-range pairs receive the hairpin LJ interaction, with parameters
\begin{align}
    \epsilon_{\mathrm{hp}}=5.0,
    \quad
    \sigma_{\mathrm{hp}}=0.9,
    \quad
    \delta_{\mathrm{hp}}=0.3,
    \nonumber \\
    r_{\mathrm{cut}}=3.0,
    \quad
    s_{\min}=2.
\end{align}
The hairpin configuration also enables excluded volume and centered confinement.
The confinement potential is
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{conf}}(x)
    =
    c
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \|x_i-\bar{x}\|^2,
    \qquad
    c=0.02,
\end{equation}
where $\bar{x}$ denotes the chain center of mass.
Together, these terms create a stable but thermally fluctuating structure, the closest of our benchmarks to the protein motivation: the model must learn a distribution shaped by stabilizing nonlocal contacts \cite{honda2004,satoh2006}.

\section{Continuous Normalizing Flow}

The CNF learns an ODE mapping data coordinates to a standard normal base distribution \cite{chen2018,grathwohl2018}.
Let $z(\tau)$ denote the state at pseudotime $\tau \in [0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dz(\tau)}{d\tau} = f_\theta(z(\tau), \tau).
\end{equation}
Here $\tau$ is a generative-model coordinate, not physical Brownian time.
The CNF integrates from data to noise for likelihood evaluation and from noise to data for sampling.
Unlike the diffusion model, which fixes the forward noising SDE and learns only the reverse-time score, the CNF learns the continuous transport velocity $f_\theta$ directly.

The log-density evolves according to the instantaneous change-of-variables formula:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{d\tau}\log p(z(\tau))
    =
    -\operatorname{Tr}
    \left(
        \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z}
    \right).
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the accumulated log-density change satisfies
\begin{align}
    \log P(z(\tau)) - \log P(z(0))
    &=
    \Delta \log P_z(\tau)
    \nonumber \\
    &=
    \int_0^\tau
    -
    \operatorname{Tr}
    \left(
        \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z(\lambda)}
    \right)
    d\lambda.
\end{align}
For a training sample $x$, we solve
\begin{align}
    z(0) &= x, \\
    z(1) &= \mathrm{ODESolve}(z(0), f_\theta, 0, 1).
\end{align}
The log likelihood is then
\begin{equation}
    \log p_\theta(x)
    =
    \log \mathcal{N}(z(1); 0, I)
    -
    \Delta \log p,
    \end{equation}
where the ODE solve accumulates $\Delta \log p$.
Equivalently, the augmented ODE system is
\begin{align}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        z(1) \\
        \Delta \log P_z(1)
    \end{bmatrix}
    &=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        z(0)=x \\
        \Delta \log P_z(0)=0
    \end{bmatrix}
    \nonumber \\
    &\quad
    +
    \int_0^1
    \begin{bmatrix}
        f_\theta(z(\tau),\tau) \\
        -\operatorname{Tr}
        \left(
            \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z(\tau)}
        \right)
    \end{bmatrix}
    d\tau.
\end{align}
The training objective minimizes negative log likelihood:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CNF}}(\theta)
    =
    -\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}
    \left[
        \log p_\theta(x)
    \right].
\end{equation}

We parameterize the CNF with an EGNN-style vector field that builds pairwise messages from node embeddings, sequence separation $i-j$, squared pair distances $\|x_i-x_j\|^2$, and pseudotime $\tau$.
Coordinate updates use relative displacements, keeping the vector field translation equivariant after centering.
We estimate the divergence with a Hutchinson JVP trace estimator \cite{hutchinson1989,grathwohl2018}.
Sampling starts from Gaussian noise and integrates backward from $\tau=1$ to $\tau=0$.

\subsection*{Adjoint Sensitivity Equations}

Parameter gradients can be computed with an adjoint ODE.
For the scalar objective
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}
    =
    \log P(z(1)) - \Delta \log P_z(1),
\end{equation}
define the adjoint state
\begin{equation}
    a(\tau)
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        a_z(\tau) \\
        a_\Delta(\tau)
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \partial \mathcal{L}/\partial z \\
        \partial \mathcal{L}/\partial(\Delta \log P_z)
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
At $\tau=1$,
\begin{equation}
    a(1)
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \nabla_z \log P(z(1)) \\
        -1
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The adjoint dynamics satisfy
\begin{align}
    \frac{da(\tau)}{d\tau}
    &=
    -
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z} & 0 \\
        -\nabla_z
        \operatorname{Tr}
        \left(
            \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z}
        \right)
        & 0
    \end{bmatrix}^{T}
    a(\tau),
    \\
    \frac{d}{d\tau}
    \left(
        \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta}
    \right)
    &=
    a(\tau)^T
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial \theta} \\
        -\nabla_\theta
        \operatorname{Tr}
        \left(
            \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z}
        \right)
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
We delegate the differentiable ODE solves and adjoint machinery to the SciML stack.

\section{Variance-Preserving Diffusion Model}

The diffusion model learns the score field directly \cite{ho2020,song2021}.
We define a continuous-time variance-preserving (VP) forward SDE
\begin{equation}
    dx_t
    =
    -\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)x_t\,dt
    +
    \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dw_t,
\end{equation}
with a linear noise-rate schedule
\begin{equation}
    \beta(t) = \beta_{\min} + t(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min}),
    \quad
    t \in [0,1],
\end{equation}
with $\beta_{\min}=0.1$ and $\beta_{\max}=20$ in our experiments.
The marginal forward process has the form
\begin{equation}
    x_t = \mu(t)x_0 + \sigma(t)\epsilon,
    \quad
    \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
    \mu(t)
    &=
    \exp
    \left[
        -\frac{1}{4}(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})t^2
        -
        \frac{1}{2}\beta_{\min}t
    \right], \\
    \sigma^2(t)
    &=
    1
    -
    \exp
    \left[
        -\frac{1}{2}(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})t^2
        -
        \beta_{\min}t
    \right].
\end{align}
We center both $x_0$ and the sampled noise so the diffusion process stays in the centered subspace used for training.
For numerical stability, we clamp training times below $10^{-5}$ and floor $\sigma(t)$ at the corresponding minimum positive value before forming the score target.

The score network $s_\theta(x_t,t)$ estimates
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{x_t}\log p_{0t}(x_t|x_0)
    =
    -\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma(t)}.
\end{equation}
We minimize the weighted denoising score-matching objective \cite{ho2020,song2021}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}(\theta)
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{t,x_0,\epsilon}
    \left[
        \sigma^2(t)
        \left\|
            s_\theta(x_t,t)
            +
            \frac{\epsilon}{\sigma(t)}
        \right\|_2^2
    \right].
\end{equation}

The diffusion score model uses the same EGNN-style architecture with learned node embeddings, sequence-distance and radial pair features, and pairwise coordinate updates.
We center the output and interpret it as $\nabla_x \log p_t(x)$.

Sampling follows the reverse-time VP SDE \cite{song2021}.
For the linear schedule, the reverse coefficients are
\begin{equation}
    f(t) = -\frac{1}{2}\beta(t),
    \quad
    g(t)^2 = \beta(t).
\end{equation}
An Euler-Maruyama sampler starts from centered Gaussian noise at $t=1$ and steps backward:
\begin{align}
    x_{t-\Delta t}
    &=
    x_t
    -
    \left[
        f(t)x_t
        -
        g(t)^2 s_\theta(x_t,t)
    \right]\Delta t
    \nonumber \\
    &\quad
    +
    g(t)\sqrt{\Delta t}\,z,
    \qquad
    z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I).
\end{align}
The sampler integrates from $t=1$ to $t=10^{-4}$, clipping score norms to $64$, recentering each state, and omitting the final noise draw.
During training, we clip parameter gradients elementwise to magnitude $1$.

\section{Evaluation}

We evaluate both sample-level statistics and score-field diagnostics.
For sample quality, we compare average pairwise distances between generated and training configurations.

The simulator provides analytic force targets for all Rouse systems.
We obtain the CNF score by differentiating the learned log likelihood,
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_x \log p_\theta(x),
\end{equation}
while we take the diffusion score from the network output at a small time near zero.
We compare these learned scores against the known Brownian target $F(x)/D$ \cite{arts2023}.
This benchmark is stricter than visual or distributional sample comparison: the model must recover the local equilibrium score, not only generate plausible conformations.

The Gaussian baseline samples centered independent noise and serves only as a structural reference; it does not define a learned force field.

\section{Julia and SciML Implementation}

We implement the project in Julia using the SciML ecosystem \cite{bezanson2017,rackauckas2017,rackauckas2020universal}.
This stack lets us combine stochastic simulation, neural ODE likelihoods, differentiable ODE solves, GPU array code, and trace estimation in a single code path without translating between separate frameworks.

\subsection*{Brownian Data Generation with StochasticDiffEq}

We construct the Rouse data generator as a SciML SDE problem \cite{rouse1953,rackauckas2017}.
We represent
\begin{equation}
    dX_t = F(X_t)\,dt + \sqrt{2D}\,dW_t
\end{equation}
as an \texttt{SDEProblem} whose drift calls the same polymer force routine used for score targets.
We solve with \texttt{StochasticDiffEq}, choosing among Euler-Maruyama, Euler-Heun, RKMil, SOSRA, and SOSRA2 via YAML configuration.
Saved trajectories are loaded through HDF5, separating simulation from model training while keeping both stages in Julia.

\subsection*{CNF Likelihoods as SciML ODE Problems}

The CNF is an \texttt{ODEProblem} whose \texttt{ComponentArray} state contains coordinates and accumulated log-density change:
\begin{equation}
    u(\tau)
    =
    \left(
        x(\tau),
        \Delta \log p(\tau)
    \right).
\end{equation}
The ODE right-hand side returns
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{d\tau}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x(\tau) \\
        \Delta \log p(\tau)
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        f_\theta(x(\tau),\tau) \\
        -\operatorname{Tr}
        \left(
            \partial f_\theta/\partial x
        \right)
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The same problem definition runs forward for likelihood evaluation and backward for sampling.
We use adaptive \texttt{Tsit5} (with \texttt{RK4} as an alternative) and train with \texttt{InterpolatingAdjoint(autojacvec=ZygoteVJP())} so that gradients propagate through the ODE solve.
This keeps the generative model close to its mathematical definition: the likelihood model is an ODE with a structured state, a vector field, a trace estimator, and a SciML adjoint.

\subsection*{Hutchinson JVP Trace Estimator}

We use a Hutchinson trace estimator with a Jacobian-vector product (JVP), following FFJORD \cite{grathwohl2018}.
For a vector field $f_\theta(x,\tau)$, the Hutchinson identity gives
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Tr}
    \left(
        \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial x}
    \right)
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{v}
    \left[
        v^T
        \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial x}
        v
    \right],
\end{equation}
where $v$ denotes a Rademacher probe.
We sample one probe per batch when constructing the \texttt{ODEProblem} and reuse it for every RHS evaluation in the adaptive solve.
The augmented RHS returns the coordinate velocity $f_\theta(x,\tau)$ and the log-density derivative $-\widehat{\operatorname{Tr}}$, so the solver integrates the stochastic trace estimate alongside the coordinates.
The trace estimate is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\operatorname{Tr}}
    =
    \sum_{i,j}
    v_{i,j}
    \left[
        J_{f_\theta}(x,\tau)v
    \right]_{i,j}.
\end{equation}

We propagate the JVP explicitly through the full EGNN vector field.
Each layer maintains node features $h_i^\ell$ and their directional derivatives $dh_i^\ell$ with respect to the coordinate probe $v$, initialized as $dh_i^0=0$ since the learned embeddings do not depend on coordinates.
For $r_{ij}=x_i-x_j$, the JVP carries
\begin{equation}
    dr_{ij}=v_i-v_j,
    \qquad
    d\|r_{ij}\|^2
    =
    2r_{ij}^{T}dr_{ij}.
\end{equation}
The edge input at layer $\ell$ contains
\begin{equation}
    \left(
        h_i^\ell,
        h_j^\ell,
        i-j,
        \|r_{ij}\|^2,
        \tau
    \right),
\end{equation}
while its JVP input contains
\begin{equation}
    \left(
        dh_i^\ell,
        dh_j^\ell,
        0,
        2r_{ij}^{T}dr_{ij},
        0
    \right).
\end{equation}
The helper \texttt{forward\_mlp\_generic\_jvp} propagates the value and derivative through each affine layer and \texttt{tanh} nonlinearity.
The edge network outputs a coordinate weight $w_{ij}^\ell$ and node message $m_{ij}^\ell$ along with derivatives $dw_{ij}^\ell$ and $dm_{ij}^\ell$.
The coordinate JVP for one EGNN layer follows the product rule:
\begin{equation}
    d\Delta x_i^\ell
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_j
    M_{ij}
    \left(
        dw_{ij}^\ell r_{ij}
        +
        w_{ij}^\ell dr_{ij}
    \right),
\end{equation}
where $M_{ij}$ masks self-edges.
The node-feature JVP uses the derivative of the aggregated messages,
\begin{equation}
    d\bar{m}_i^\ell
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_j
    M_{ij}dm_{ij}^\ell,
\end{equation}
passes $(dh_i^\ell,d\bar{m}_i^\ell,0)$ through the node MLP JVP, and updates $dh_i^{\ell+1}$ with the primal node features.
After all layers, we average both coordinate updates and directional derivatives by the number of EGNN layers, returning $f_\theta(x,\tau)$ and $J_{f_\theta}(x,\tau)v$ in one forward pass.
This avoids materializing the full Jacobian, which is already a $64 \times 64$ dense object for a 32-bead 2D chain, and keeps the CNF likelihood scalable.

We also provide an exact Zygote-based divergence for smaller checks and a finite-difference Hutchinson mode (\texttt{hutchinson\_fd}) that approximates the JVP by
\begin{equation}
    J_{f_\theta}(x,\tau)v
    \approx
    \frac{
        f_\theta(x+\epsilon v,\tau)
        -
        f_\theta(x-\epsilon v,\tau)
    }{2\epsilon}.
\end{equation}
This finite-difference path serves as a diagnostic fallback.
The production path (\texttt{hutchinson\_jvp}) is faster and avoids step-size error because the directional derivative is exact.
Training still uses Zygote for gradients through the CNF loss and SciML adjoint solve, but we implement the EGNN JVP explicitly rather than relying on automatic differentiation.

\subsection*{Autodiff and Stability}

\section{Overdamped Trajectory-Inference Algorithm}

The trajectory-inference procedure is as follows.
First, obtain equilibrium snapshots
\begin{equation}
    \{\vec{q}_1,\ldots,\vec{q}_n\}
    \sim
    P(\vec{q}).
\end{equation}
Train a CNF to model $P(\vec{q})$ and evaluate $\log P(\vec{q})$.
At each physical timestep, compute the gradient of the CNF log density:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{\vec{q}} \log P(\vec{q}(t)).
\end{equation}
Under detailed balance, this score determines the overdamped drift:
\begin{equation}
    F(\vec{q})
    =
    D\nabla_{\vec{q}}\log P(\vec{q}).
\end{equation}
Then evolve an overdamped stochastic trajectory:
\begin{equation}
    d\vec{q}_t
    =
    D\nabla_{\vec{q}}\log P(\vec{q}_t)\,dt
    +
    \sqrt{2D}\,dW_t.
\end{equation}
This loop matches the Brownian setting used in our experiments.
We do not yet apply it to atomistic proteins; we validate the score-learning components on Brownian/Rouse systems where the target force field is known.

For SDEs, $dW_t$ denotes a Brownian increment with mean zero and variance $dt$; terms proportional to $dW_t$ create fluctuations of size $\sqrt{dt}$, while drift terms proportional to $dt$ create deterministic motion.

\section{Boltzmann Score and Conservative Forces}

At equilibrium, an atomistic configuration $\vec{q}$ with potential energy $V(\vec{q})$ follows the Boltzmann density
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        P(\vec{q})
        &=
        \frac{1}{Z}
        \exp
        \left(
            -\frac{V(\vec{q})}{k_B T}
        \right),
        \\
        Z
        &=
        \int
        \exp
        \left(
            -\frac{V(\vec{q})}{k_B T}
        \right)
        d\vec{q}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This assumes the canonical ensemble at fixed temperature after marginalizing over momenta.
Taking the logarithm gives
\begin{equation}
    \log P(\vec{q})
    =
    -\frac{V(\vec{q})}{k_B T}
    -
    \log Z.
\end{equation}
The partition function $Z$ does not depend on $\vec{q}$, so differentiating with respect to the coordinates yields
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{\vec{q}}\log P(\vec{q})
    =
    -\frac{1}{k_B T}
    \nabla_{\vec{q}}V(\vec{q}).
\end{equation}
Rearranging gives the force
\begin{equation}
    -\nabla_{\vec{q}}V(\vec{q})
    =
    k_B T
    \nabla_{\vec{q}}\log P(\vec{q}).
\end{equation}
Any differentiable model of the equilibrium log density therefore provides a conservative force estimate when multiplied by $k_B T$.
The force field is conservative because it derives from the scalar potential $-k_B T\log P(\vec{q})$.

\section{Overdamped Brownian Score Relation}

Our Brownian systems follow the Itô SDE
\begin{equation}
    dX_t
    =
    F(X_t)\,dt
    +
    \sqrt{2D}\,dW_t.
\end{equation}
The corresponding Fokker-Planck equation for the density $p(x,t)$ is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial p}{\partial t}
    =
    -\nabla_x \cdot \left(F(x)p(x,t)\right)
    +
    D\nabla_x^2 p(x,t).
\end{equation}
At stationarity, $\partial p/\partial t=0$.
Writing the probability current as
\begin{equation}
    J(x)
    =
    F(x)p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)
    -
    D\nabla_x p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x),
\end{equation}
Detailed balance sets $J(x)=0$ at equilibrium---a stronger condition than stationarity, which only requires $\nabla_x\cdot J(x)=0$ and permits circulating currents.
Our Rouse score target assumes this reversible case, so
\begin{equation}
    F(x)p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)
    =
    D\nabla_x p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x).
\end{equation}
Dividing by $p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)$ gives
\begin{equation}
    F(x)
    =
    D
    \frac{\nabla_x p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)}
         {p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)}
    =
    D\nabla_x\log p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x).
\end{equation}
Thus the equilibrium score target for the Brownian simulations is
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_x\log p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)
    =
    \frac{F(x)}{D}.
\end{equation}
For the base Rouse experiments, $D=0.25$, so the score target becomes $4F(x)$.

\section{Rouse-Chain Score}

For the base Rouse chain, the dimensionless equilibrium potential is
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{bond}}(x)
    =
    \frac{k_\xi}{2D}
    \sum_{i=2}^{N}
    \|x_i-x_{i-1}\|^2.
\end{equation}
The equilibrium density obeys
\begin{equation}
    p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)
    \propto
    \exp\left[-U_{\mathrm{bond}}(x)\right],
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{x_i}\log p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)
    =
    -
    \nabla_{x_i}U_{\mathrm{bond}}(x).
\end{equation}
For an interior bead, $1<i<N$, only the two adjacent bonds contribute:
\begin{align}
    \nabla_{x_i}U_{\mathrm{bond}}(x)
    &=
    \frac{k_\xi}{2D}
    \nabla_{x_i}
    \left(
        \|x_i-x_{i-1}\|^2
        +
        \|x_{i+1}-x_i\|^2
    \right)
    \nonumber \\
    &=
    \frac{k_\xi}{D}
    \left(
        2x_i-x_{i-1}-x_{i+1}
    \right).
\end{align}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{x_i}\log p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)
    =
    \frac{k_\xi}{D}
    \left(
        x_{i+1}-2x_i+x_{i-1}
    \right),
\end{equation}
and the Brownian drift becomes
\begin{equation}
    F_i(x)
    =
    D\nabla_{x_i}\log p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)
    =
    k_\xi
    \left(
        x_{i+1}-2x_i+x_{i-1}
    \right),
\end{equation}
with one-sided endpoint modifications.
Explicitly, the endpoints satisfy
\begin{align}
    F_1(x)
    &=
    k_\xi(x_2-x_1),
    \\
    F_N(x)
    &=
    k_\xi(x_{N-1}-x_N).
\end{align}
The Rouse potential is translation-invariant, so we center every configuration before training and evaluation to remove the zero mode.

The ring-closure experiment adds one harmonic bond between beads $1$ and $N$:
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{ring}}(x)
    =
    U_{\mathrm{bond}}(x)
    +
    \frac{k_\xi}{2D}
    \|x_1-x_N\|^2.
\end{equation}
Only the two terminal beads receive new bonded score contributions:
\begin{align}
    s^{\mathrm{ring}}_1(x)
    &=
    \frac{k_\xi}{D}
    (x_N-x_1),
    \\
    s^{\mathrm{ring}}_N(x)
    &=
    \frac{k_\xi}{D}
    (x_1-x_N).
\end{align}
We treat the $(1,N)$ pair as bonded for nonbonded exclusions.
The ring configuration also enables excluded-volume and centered-confinement terms but leaves the optional ring Lennard-Jones contact disabled.

The hairpin experiment adds pair potentials.
For a pair $(i,j)$, define
\begin{equation}
    r_{ij}=x_i-x_j,
    \qquad
    \rho_{ij}^2
    =
    \|r_{ij}\|^2+\delta^2,
\end{equation}
where $\delta$ is the softening length used in the simulator.
Cutoffs and bonded-neighbor exclusions select which pairs contribute.
For any active pair potential $U_{ij}(\rho_{ij})$, the pair score on bead $i$ is
\begin{equation}
    s_{i\leftarrow j}(x)
    =
    -\nabla_{x_i}U_{ij},
    \qquad
    s_{j\leftarrow i}(x)
    =
    -s_{i\leftarrow j}(x).
\end{equation}

Excluded volume uses the repulsive power-law potential
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{EV},ij}
    =
    \epsilon_{\mathrm{EV}}
    \left(
        \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{EV}}}
             {\rho_{ij}}
    \right)^p.
\end{equation}
Differentiating gives the score contribution
\begin{equation}
    s^{\mathrm{EV}}_{i\leftarrow j}
    =
    \epsilon_{\mathrm{EV}}
    p
    \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{EV}}^p}
         {\rho_{ij}^{p+2}}
    r_{ij}.
\end{equation}
The ring-closure and hairpin runs use the defaults
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \epsilon_{\mathrm{EV}}
        &=
        0.03,
        &
        \sigma_{\mathrm{EV}}
        &=
        1.0,
        &
        p
        &=
        12,
        \\
        \delta
        &=
        0.3,
        &
        r_{\mathrm{cut}}
        &=
        1.5.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and exclude bonded neighbors.

The Lennard-Jones potential used for attractive hairpin contacts is
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{LJ},ij}
    =
    4\epsilon
    \left[
        \left(
            \frac{\sigma}
                 {\rho_{ij}}
        \right)^{12}
        -
        \left(
            \frac{\sigma}
                 {\rho_{ij}}
        \right)^6
    \right].
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}
    a_{ij}
    =
    \frac{\sigma^2}
         {\rho_{ij}^2},
    \qquad
    a_{ij}^3
    =
    \left(
        \frac{\sigma}
             {\rho_{ij}}
    \right)^6,
    \qquad
    a_{ij}^6
    =
    \left(
        \frac{\sigma}
             {\rho_{ij}}
    \right)^{12}.
\end{equation}
Then the LJ score contribution is
\begin{equation}
    s^{\mathrm{LJ}}_{i\leftarrow j}
    =
    24\epsilon
    \frac{1}{\rho_{ij}^2}
    \left(
        2a_{ij}^6-a_{ij}^3
    \right)
    r_{ij}.
\end{equation}
Optionally subtracting the LJ value at the cutoff shifts the potential by a constant and does not change the score.

    \mathcal{H}

\end{equation}
with

        &=
        0.3,

        r_{\mathrm{cut}}
        &=
        3.0,
        &
        s_{\min}
        &=
        2.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Both systems also enable centered confinement.
The chain center of mass is
\begin{equation}
    \bar{x}
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    x_i
\end{equation}
and the potential
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{conf}}(x)
    =
    c
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \|x_i-\bar{x}\|^2.
\end{equation}
Rewriting the centered spread as
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \|x_i-\bar{x}\|^2
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \|x_i\|^2
    -
    N\|\bar{x}\|^2.
\end{equation}
and differentiating with respect to bead $i$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{x_i}U_{\mathrm{conf}}(x)
    =
    2c(x_i-\bar{x}),
\end{equation}
so the confinement score contribution is
\begin{equation}
    s^{\mathrm{conf}}_i(x)
    =
    -\nabla_{x_i}U_{\mathrm{conf}}(x)
    =
    -2c(x_i-\bar{x}).
\end{equation}
This pulls each bead toward the instantaneous center of mass without pinning the polymer to the origin (translating all beads leaves $x_i-\bar{x}$ unchanged).
Both configurations use
\begin{equation}
    c=0.02
\end{equation}
In general, if the total dimensionless potential is $U(x)$, the analytic equilibrium score is
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_x\log p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)
    =
    -\nabla_x U(x),
\end{equation}
and the corresponding Brownian drift is
\begin{equation}
    F(x)
    =
    -D\nabla_x U(x).
\end{equation}
This combined expression is the target produced by \texttt{polymer\_langevin\_score!}; multiplying it by $D$ gives the Brownian drift used in simulation.

\section{Continuous Normalizing Flow Log Density}

The CNF defines an invertible flow by the ODE \cite{chen2018,grathwohl2018}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dz(\tau)}{d\tau}
    =
    f_\theta(z(\tau),\tau).
\end{equation}
Let $p(z(\tau),\tau)$ denote the density of states at pseudotime $\tau$.
Probability conservation gives the continuity equation
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial p}{\partial \tau}
    +
    \nabla_z \cdot
    \left(
        p f_\theta
    \right)
    =
    0.
\end{equation}
Expanding the divergence via the product rule,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \nabla_z\cdot
        \left(
            p f_\theta
        \right)
        &=
        \sum_m
        \frac{\partial}
             {\partial z_m}
        \left(
            p f_{\theta,m}
        \right)
        \\
        &=
        \sum_m
        \left[
            f_{\theta,m}
            \frac{\partial p}{\partial z_m}
            +
            p
            \frac{\partial f_{\theta,m}}
                 {\partial z_m}
        \right]
        \\
        &=
        f_\theta\cdot\nabla_z p
        +
        p
        \operatorname{Tr}
        \left(
            \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z}
        \right).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{Tr}(\partial f_\theta/\partial z)=\sum_m \partial f_{\theta,m}/\partial z_m$ is the Jacobian trace.
Substituting gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial p}{\partial \tau}
    +
    f_\theta \cdot \nabla_z p
    +
    p
    \operatorname{Tr}
    \left(
        \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z}
    \right)
    =
    0.
\end{equation}
Along a trajectory $z(\tau)$, the chain rule gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{d\tau}p(z(\tau),\tau)
    =
    \frac{\partial p}{\partial \tau}
    +
    \frac{dz}{d\tau}
    \cdot
    \nabla_z p.
\end{equation}
Substituting the CNF ODE, $dz/d\tau=f_\theta(z(\tau),\tau)$, yields
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{d\tau}p(z(\tau),\tau)
    =
    \frac{\partial p}{\partial \tau}
    +
    f_\theta
    \cdot
    \nabla_z p.
\end{equation}
Combining with the continuity equation gives
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \frac{d}{d\tau}p(z(\tau),\tau)
        &+
        p(z(\tau),\tau)
        \operatorname{Tr}
        \left(
            \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z}
        \right)
        =
        0,
        \\
        \frac{d}{d\tau}p(z(\tau),\tau)
        &=
        -
        p(z(\tau),\tau)
        \operatorname{Tr}
        \left(
            \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z}
        \right).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Dividing by $p$ gives the instantaneous change-of-variables formula
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{d\tau}\log p(z(\tau),\tau)
    =
    -
    \operatorname{Tr}
    \left(
        \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z}
    \right).
\end{equation}
Integrating from $\tau=0$ to $\tau=1$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \log p(z(1),1)
    -
    \log p(z(0),0)
    =
    -
    \int_0^1
    \operatorname{Tr}
    \left(
        \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z(\tau)}
    \right)
    d\tau.
\end{equation}
With $z(0)=x$ and standard normal base density at $z(1)$,
\begin{equation}
    \log p_\theta(x)
    =
    \log \mathcal{N}(z(1);0,I)
    +
    \int_0^1
    \operatorname{Tr}
    \left(
        \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z(\tau)}
    \right)
    d\tau.
\end{equation}
The augmented state stores $\Delta\log p(\tau)=\log p(z(\tau),\tau)-\log p(z(0),0)$, so we accumulate
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \frac{d}{d\tau}\Delta\log p
        &=
        -
        \operatorname{Tr}
        \left(
            \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial z}
        \right),
        \\
        \Delta\log p(0)
        &=
        0,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and evaluates
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \log p_\theta(x)
        &=
        \log
        \mathcal{N}(z(1);0,I)
        \\
        &\quad
        -
        \Delta\log p(1).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{Hutchinson Trace Estimator}

Let $A$ denote the Jacobian matrix $\partial f_\theta/\partial x$.
The CNF log-density equation needs the divergence of the vector field,
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_x\cdot f_\theta(x,\tau)
    =
    \operatorname{Tr}
    \left(
        \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial x}
    \right).
\end{equation}
For large systems, forming the full Jacobian is wasteful since only this scalar trace is needed.
Hutchinson's estimator recovers it from a random probe and one Jacobian-vector product \cite{hutchinson1989,roostakhorasani2015}.
For a Rademacher probe $v$ with independent entries satisfying
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[v_i]=0,
    \qquad
    \mathbb{E}[v_i v_j]=\delta_{ij},
\end{equation}
we have
\begin{align}
    \mathbb{E}\left[v^T A v\right]
    &=
    \mathbb{E}
    \left[
        \sum_i\sum_j v_i A_{ij} v_j
    \right]
    \nonumber \\
    &=
    \sum_i\sum_j
    A_{ij}
    \mathbb{E}\left[v_i v_j\right]
    \nonumber \\
    &=
    \sum_i A_{ii}
    =
    \operatorname{Tr}(A).
\end{align}
Thus
\begin{equation}
    v^T
    \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial x}
    v
\end{equation}
is an unbiased estimate of the divergence.
Component by component, $Av$ has entries
\begin{equation}
    (Av)_i
    =
    \sum_j
    A_{ij}v_j,
\end{equation}
so the scalar Hutchinson estimate is
\begin{equation}
    v^TAv
    =
    \sum_i
    v_i(Av)_i
    =
    \sum_i\sum_j
    A_{ij}v_i v_j.
\end{equation}
Off-diagonal terms vanish in expectation ($\mathbb{E}[v_iv_j]=0$ for $i\neq j$) and diagonal terms survive ($v_i^2=1$):
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[v^TAv]
    =
    \sum_i
    A_{ii}.
\end{equation}
For bead coordinates, index $i$ represents a bead-coordinate pair $(a,k)$, so
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Tr}(A)
    =
    \sum_{a,k}
    \frac{\partial f_{\theta,a,k}}
         {\partial x_{a,k}}.
\end{equation}

In our implementation, we sample one $\{-1,+1\}$ probe per batch and fix it for the entire ODE solve.
Drawing a new probe at every RHS call would introduce spurious time-dependent noise.
For each RHS call, the augmented derivative has the schematic form
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{d\tau}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x \\
        \Delta\log p
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        f_\theta(x,\tau) \\
        -
        v^T J_{f_\theta}(x,\tau)v
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
We compute the matrix-vector product
\begin{equation}
    J_{f_\theta}(x,\tau)v
    =
    \left.
    \frac{d}{d\alpha}
    f_\theta(x+\alpha v,\tau)
    \right|_{\alpha=0}
\end{equation}
analytically for the EGNN, then form
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\operatorname{Tr}}
    =
    \sum_{i,k}
    v_{i,k}
    \left[
        J_{f_\theta}(x,\tau)v
    \right]_{i,k},
\end{equation}
where $i$ indexes beads and $k$ indexes spatial coordinates.
During training, the loss differentiates through this stochastic trace estimate with respect to the network parameters.

\section{Full EGNN JVP Recursion}

We write the analytic JVP implemented by \texttt{\_egnn\_velocity\_jvp}.
This differentiates the vector field with respect to coordinates in the probe direction $v$ (not with respect to parameters; Zygote handles parameter gradients separately).
The notation $d(\cdot)$ denotes the directional derivative along $x+\alpha v$:
\begin{equation}
    dQ
    =
    \left.
    \frac{d}{d\alpha}
    Q(x+\alpha v)
    \right|_{\alpha=0}.
\end{equation}
We propagate $d(\cdot)$ quantities through the EGNN operations; the final result is $J_{f_\theta}(x,\tau)v$.
The initialization is
\begin{equation}
    dx_i=v_i,
    \qquad
    dh_i^0=0.
\end{equation}
The learned embeddings do not depend on coordinates, so $dh_i^0=0$.
The pairwise quantities are
\begin{equation}
    r_{ij}=x_i-x_j,
    \qquad
    d_{ij}=\|r_{ij}\|^2,
\end{equation}
with directional derivatives
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        dr_{ij}
        &=
        dx_i-dx_j
        =
        v_i-v_j,
        \\
        dd_{ij}
        &=
        d(r_{ij}^T r_{ij})
        =
        2r_{ij}^Tdr_{ij}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The edge network receives
\begin{equation}
    e_{ij}^\ell
    =
    \left(
        h_i^\ell,
        h_j^\ell,
        i-j,
        d_{ij},
        \tau
    \right)
\end{equation}
and directional input
\begin{equation}
    de_{ij}^\ell
    =
    \left(
        dh_i^\ell,
        dh_j^\ell,
        0,
        dd_{ij},
        0
    \right).
\end{equation}
Sequence separation and pseudotime do not depend on $x$, so their derivatives are zero.
The MLP JVP applies the chain rule layer by layer:
\begin{align}
    a &= W y + b,
    &
    da &= W\,dy,
    \\
    y^+ &= \tanh(a),
    &
    dy^+ &= \left(1-\tanh^2(a)\right)\odot da.
\end{align}
The final layer omits the nonlinearity.
The edge MLP returns
\begin{equation}
    (w_{ij}^\ell,m_{ij}^\ell),
    \qquad
    (dw_{ij}^\ell,dm_{ij}^\ell),
\end{equation}
where $w_{ij}^\ell$ denotes the scalar coordinate weight and $m_{ij}^\ell$ denotes the node message.
where $w_{ij}^\ell$ is the scalar coordinate weight and $m_{ij}^\ell$ is the node message.
With self-edge mask $M_{ij}$, the coordinate update is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta x_i^\ell
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_j
    M_{ij}
    w_{ij}^\ell r_{ij}.
\end{equation}
The JVP follows from the product rule:
\begin{equation}
    d\Delta x_i^\ell
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_j
    M_{ij}
    \left(
        dw_{ij}^\ell r_{ij}
        +
        w_{ij}^\ell dr_{ij}
    \right).
\end{equation}
The node message aggregation is
\begin{equation}
    \bar{m}_i^\ell
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_j
    M_{ij}m_{ij}^\ell,
    \qquad
    d\bar{m}_i^\ell
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_j
    M_{ij}dm_{ij}^\ell.
\end{equation}
The node network receives $(h_i^\ell,\bar{m}_i^\ell,\tau)$ and JVP input $(dh_i^\ell,d\bar{m}_i^\ell,0)$.
If the node MLP returns $\Delta h_i^\ell$ and $d\Delta h_i^\ell$, then
\begin{equation}
    h_i^{\ell+1}
    =
    h_i^\ell+\Delta h_i^\ell,
    \qquad
    dh_i^{\ell+1}
    =
    dh_i^\ell+d\Delta h_i^\ell.
\end{equation}
After repeating for $L$ layers, we return
\begin{equation}
    f_\theta(x,\tau)
    =
    \frac{1}{L}
    \sum_{\ell=1}^{L}
    \Delta x^\ell,
    \qquad
    J_{f_\theta}(x,\tau)v
    =
    \frac{1}{L}
    \sum_{\ell=1}^{L}
    d\Delta x^\ell.
\end{equation}
This recursion gives the full analytic JVP that the \texttt{hutchinson\_jvp} trace mode uses.

\section{Variance-Preserving Diffusion Score Target}

DDPM defines a discrete noising Markov chain \cite{sohldickstein2015,ho2020}
\begin{equation}
    q(x_k|x_{k-1})
    =
    \mathcal{N}
    \left(
        x_k;
        \sqrt{1-\beta_k}\,x_{k-1},
        \beta_k I
    \right).
\end{equation}
Here $\beta_k$ is the noise variance at step $k$.
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
    x_k
    =
    \sqrt{1-\beta_k}\,x_{k-1}
    +
    \sqrt{\beta_k}\,\epsilon_k,
    \qquad
    \epsilon_k\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).
\end{equation}
Taking $\beta_k=\beta(t)\Delta t$ and using $\sqrt{1-\beta(t)\Delta t}=1-\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$ gives
\begin{align}
    x_{t+\Delta t}-x_t
    &=
    -\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)x_t\,\Delta t
    +
    \sqrt{\beta(t)\Delta t}\,\epsilon_t
    +
    o(\Delta t).
\end{align}
The limit $\Delta t\to 0$ gives the VP forward SDE
\begin{equation}
    dx_t
    =
    -\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)x_t\,dt
    +
    \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dW_t.
\end{equation}
This continuous process preserves the DDPM structure while admitting a closed-form marginal transition density.
Define
\begin{equation}
    B(t)
    =
    \int_0^t
    \beta(s)\,ds.
\end{equation}
Multiplying by the integrating factor $e^{B(t)/2}$ (via It\^o's lemma) removes the deterministic decay:
\begin{align}
    d
    \left[
        e^{B(t)/2}x_t
    \right]
    &=
    e^{B(t)/2}
    \sqrt{\beta(t)}
    \,dw_t.
\end{align}
Integrating from $0$ to $t$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        x_t
        &=
        e^{-B(t)/2}x_0
        \\
        &\quad
        +
        \int_0^t
        e^{-(B(t)-B(s))/2}
        \sqrt{\beta(s)}
        \,dW_s.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Taking the conditional expectation gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[x_t|x_0]
    =
    e^{-B(t)/2}x_0.
\end{equation}
Writing the stochastic integral as
\begin{equation}
    \eta_t
    =
    \int_0^t
    \phi(s)\,dW_s,
    \qquad
    \phi(s)
    =
    e^{-(B(t)-B(s))/2}
    \sqrt{\beta(s)}.
\end{equation}
we have $x_t=e^{-B(t)/2}x_0+\eta_t$ with $\mathbb{E}[\eta_t|x_0]=0$.
The conditional variance is
\begin{align}
    \operatorname{Var}
    \left[
        x_t
        \middle|x_0
    \right]
    &=
    \mathbb{E}
    \left[
        x_t^2
        \middle|x_0
    \right]
    -
    \mathbb{E}
    \left[
        x_t
        \middle|x_0
    \right]^2
    \nonumber \\
    &=
    \mathbb{E}
    \left[
        \left(
            e^{-B(t)/2}x_0+\eta_t
        \right)^2
        \middle|x_0
    \right]
    -
    e^{-B(t)}x_0^2
    \nonumber \\
    &=
    e^{-B(t)}x_0^2
    +
    2e^{-B(t)/2}x_0
    \mathbb{E}[\eta_t|x_0]
    \nonumber \\
    &\quad
    +
    \mathbb{E}[\eta_t^2|x_0]
    -
    e^{-B(t)}x_0^2
    \nonumber \\
    &=
    \mathbb{E}
    \left[
        \eta_t^2
        \middle|x_0
    \right]
    \nonumber \\
    &=
    \int_0^t
    \phi^2(s)\,ds
    \nonumber \\
    &=
    e^{-B(t)}
    \int_0^t
    e^{B(s)}
    \beta(s)\,ds
    \nonumber \\
    &=
    e^{-B(t)}
    \int_0^t
    \frac{d}{ds}
    e^{B(s)}
    \,ds
    \nonumber \\
    &=
    1-e^{-B(t)}.
\end{align}
The conditional noise variance $1-e^{-B(t)}$ complements the squared signal coefficient $e^{-B(t)}$, which motivates the name variance-preserving.
For a fixed initial condition $x_0$, the marginal transition distribution takes Gaussian form
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        p_{0t}(x_t|x_0)
        &=
        \mathcal{N}
        \left(
            x_t;
            \mu(t)x_0,
            \sigma^2(t)I
        \right).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We can write the solution as
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        x_t
        &=
        \mu(t)x_0
        +
        \sigma(t)\epsilon,
        \\
        \epsilon
        &\sim
        \mathcal{N}(0,I),
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mu(t)
        &=
        e^{-B(t)/2},
        \\
        \sigma^2(t)
        &=
        1-\mu^2(t).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For the linear schedule $\beta(t)=\beta_{\min}+t(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})$,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \int_0^t\beta(s)\,ds
        &=
        \beta_{\min}t
        \\
        &\quad
        +
        \frac{1}{2}
        \left(
            \beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min}
        \right)t^2,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
which gives the $\mu(t)$ and $\sigma(t)$ expressions used in the main text.
The conditional log density satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \log p_{0t}(x_t|x_0)
        &=
        -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2(t)}
        \left\|
            x_t-\mu(t)x_0
        \right\|^2
        \\
        &\quad
        +
        C(t),
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $C(t)$ does not depend on $x_t$.
Differentiating with respect to $x_t$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \nabla_{x_t}\log p_{0t}(x_t|x_0)
        &=
        -
        \frac{x_t-\mu(t)x_0}{\sigma^2(t)}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Using $x_t-\mu(t)x_0=\sigma(t)\epsilon$ gives the denoising score target
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \nabla_{x_t}\log p_{0t}(x_t|x_0)
        &=
        -
        \frac{\epsilon}
             {\sigma(t)}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The left side is the conditional score,
\begin{equation}
    s_{0t}(x_t|x_0)
    =
    \nabla_{x_t}\log p_{0t}(x_t|x_0),
\end{equation}
the score when the clean starting point $x_0$ is known.
The reverse SDE requires the marginal score $\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t)$, which averages over all clean configurations that could produce the same noisy point:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t)
        &=
        \frac{\nabla_{x_t}p_t(x_t)}
             {p_t(x_t)}
        \\
        &=
        \frac{
            \int
            p_0(x_0)
            \nabla_{x_t}p_{0t}(x_t|x_0)
            \,dx_0
        }
        {p_t(x_t)}
        \\
        &=
        \mathbb{E}
        \left[
            \nabla_{x_t}\log p_{0t}(x_t|x_0)
            \middle|x_t
        \right].
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Training on pairs $(x_0,x_t)$ with target $-\epsilon/\sigma(t)$ therefore teaches the network the marginal score needed by the reverse sampler.
The training objective weights the squared error by $\sigma^2(t)$:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}(\theta)
        &=
        \mathbb{E}
        \left[
            \sigma^2(t)
            \left\|
                s_\theta(x_t,t)
                +
                \frac{\epsilon}{\sigma(t)}
            \right\|_2^2
        \right].
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{Fokker-Planck Time Reversal}

To see why the score appears in the reverse SDE, start from a general forward diffusion \cite{anderson1982,song2021}
\begin{equation}
    dX_t
    =
    f(X_t,t)\,dt
    +
    g(t)\,dW_t
\end{equation}
with density $p_t(x)$.
Its Fokker-Planck equation is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t}
    =
    -
    \nabla_x\cdot
    \left[
        f(x,t)p_t(x)
    \right]
    +
    \frac{1}{2}
    g^2(t)\Delta_x p_t(x).
\end{equation}
Equivalently, in terms of the probability current,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t}
        &=
        -
        \nabla_x\cdot J_t(x),
        \\
        J_t(x)
        &=
        f(x,t)p_t(x)
        -
        \frac{1}{2}
        g^2(t)\nabla_x p_t(x).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Now define the backward-time process $Y_s=X_{T-s}$ and write its density as $q_s(y)=p_{T-s}(y)$.
Because $t=T-s$,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial q_s}{\partial s}
    =
    -
    \frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t}
    =
    \nabla_y\cdot J_t(y).
\end{equation}
If $Y_s$ follows
\begin{equation}
    dY_s
    =
    \tilde f(Y_s,s)\,ds
    +
    g(T-s)\,d\bar W_s,
\end{equation}
then its Fokker-Planck equation is
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \frac{\partial q_s}{\partial s}
        &=
        -
        \nabla_y\cdot
        \left[
            \tilde f(y,s)q_s(y)
        \right]
        \\
        &\quad
        +
        \frac{1}{2}
        g^2(t)\Delta_y q_s(y).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Substituting $q_s(y)=p_t(y)$ into this backward-time Fokker-Planck equation gives
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \frac{\partial q_s}{\partial s}
        &=
        -
        \nabla_y\cdot
        \left[
            \tilde f(y,s)p_t(y)
        \right]
        \\
        &\quad
        +
        \frac{1}{2}
        g^2(t)\Delta_y p_t(y).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The reversed density must also satisfy $\partial_s q_s=\nabla_y\cdot J_t(y)$.
Using the forward current $J_t(y)=f(y,t)p_t(y)-\frac{1}{2}g^2(t)\nabla_y p_t(y)$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \frac{\partial q_s}{\partial s}
        &=
        \nabla_y\cdot
        \left[
            f(y,t)p_t(y)
            -
            \frac{1}{2}
            g^2(t)\nabla_y p_t(y)
        \right]
        \\
        &=
        \nabla_y\cdot
        \left[
            f(y,t)p_t(y)
        \right]
        -
        \frac{1}{2}
        g^2(t)\Delta_y p_t(y).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Equating the two expressions for $\partial_s q_s$ and moving the diffusion term gives
\begin{equation}
    -
    \nabla_y\cdot
    \left[
        \tilde f(y,s)p_t(y)
    \right]
    =
    \nabla_y\cdot
    \left[
        f(y,t)p_t(y)
        -
        g^2(t)\nabla_y p_t(y)
    \right].
\end{equation}
Thus the reverse current can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \tilde f(y,s)p_t(y)
    =
    -
    f(y,t)p_t(y)
    +
    g^2(t)\nabla_y p_t(y).
\end{equation}
Dividing by $p_t(y)$ converts the density-gradient term into the score:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde f(y,s)
    =
    -
    f(y,t)
    +
    g^2(t)\nabla_y\log p_t(y),
    \qquad
    t=T-s.
\end{equation}
Since $ds=-dt$, rewriting in the original variable with $dt<0$ flips the sign:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde f\,ds
    =
    \left[
        -
        f
        +
        g^2\nabla\log p_t
    \right](-dt)
    =
    \left[
        f
        -
        g^2\nabla\log p_t
    \right]dt.
\end{equation}
Writing $\bar W_t$ for the reverse-time Brownian motion, the reverse SDE in the original time variable is
\begin{equation}
    dX_t
    =
    \left[
        f(X_t,t)
        -
        g^2(t)
        \nabla_x\log p_t(X_t)
    \right]dt
    +
    g(t)\,d\bar W_t.
\end{equation}
The reverse VP sampler below substitutes the VP drift, diffusion coefficient, and learned score into this equation \cite{song2021}.

\section{Reverse-Time VP Sampler}

Applying the time-reversal result to the VP forward diffusion
\begin{equation}
    dx_t
    =
    f(x_t,t)\,dt
    +
    g(t)\,dw_t,
\end{equation}
with density $p_t(x)$.
with $dt<0$, the reverse drift is
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        f(x_t,t)
        -
        g^2(t)
        \nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For the VP process,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        f(x_t,t)
        &=
        -\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)x_t,
        \\
        g^2(t)
        &=
        \beta(t).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Substituting gives
\begin{equation}
    dX_t
    =
    \left[
        -\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)X_t
        -
        \beta(t)\nabla_x\log p_t(X_t)
    \right]dt
    +
    \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,d\bar W_t,
\end{equation}
where sampling uses $dt<0$.
Replacing the true score with the learned model $s_\theta(x,t)$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        -\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)x_t
        -
        \beta(t)s_\theta(x_t,t).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The Euler-Maruyama update stepping from $t$ to $t-\Delta t$ is
\begin{align}
    x_{t-\Delta t}
    &=
    x_t
    -
    \left[
        f(x_t,t)
        -
        g^2(t)s_\theta(x_t,t)
    \right]\Delta t
    \nonumber \\
    &\quad
    +
    g(t)\sqrt{\Delta t}\,z,
    \qquad
    z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).
\end{align}
With VP coefficients,
\begin{align}
    x_{t-\Delta t}
    &=
    x_t
    +
    \left[
        \frac{1}{2}\beta(t)x_t
        +
        \beta(t)s_\theta(x_t,t)
    \right]\Delta t
    \nonumber \\
    &\quad
    +
    \sqrt{\beta(t)\Delta t}\,z.
\end{align}
We recenter after each step and omit the final noise draw near $t=10^{-4}$.
